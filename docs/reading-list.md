## Week 1

**Required**

- **Relational inductive biases, deep learning, and graph networks** — Peter W. Battaglia, Jessica B. Hamrick, Victor Bapst, Alvaro Sanchez-Gonzalez, Vinicius Zambaldi, et al. (DeepMind/Google Brain/MIT/Edinburgh) (arXiv preprint, 2018) <https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.01261>
  This is the position paper that makes the week's core argument: deep learning succeeds when its architecture matches the structure of the data, and graphs are the most general way to express relational structure. Read Sections 1-2 closely and study Table 1 and Figure 2, which map fully-connected, convolutional, and recurrent layers to their relational inductive biases — that table is the single most important artifact this week. In Section 3, focus on the graph network (GN) block and its update equations as a unifying template you will see specialized in later weeks; you can skim Section 4 and the appendices on a first pass. Read it for the argument, not for experiments — there are none, and that's the point.
- **Representation Learning on Graphs: Methods and Applications** — William L. Hamilton, Rex Ying, Jure Leskovec (IEEE Data Engineering Bulletin 2017, 2017) <https://arxiv.org/abs/1709.05584>
  A readable survey that organizes the whole field around one idea: an encoder maps nodes to vectors, a decoder recovers graph structure from them, and every method is a choice of encoder, decoder, similarity measure, and loss. Internalize that four-part framework in Section 2 and the table that slots methods (DeepWalk, node2vec, GCNs) into it — it is the mental filing cabinet for the rest of the course. Notice how the ML tasks from lecture (node classification, link prediction, clustering) become downstream uses of embeddings, and how the adjacency matrix and Laplacian from lecture appear inside the methods. Skim the sections on specific neighborhood-aggregation architectures lightly; we return to them in depth in the GNN weeks.
**Optional**

- **A Gentle Introduction to Graph Neural Networks** — Benjamin Sanchez-Lengeling, Emily Reif, Adam Pearce, Alexander B. Wiltschko (Distill 2021, 2021) <https://doi.org/10.23915/distill.00033>
  An interactive, visual article — the friendliest on-ramp to this week's material, worth opening before the two required papers if the lecture felt abstract. Play with the widgets showing how images, text, and molecules become graphs, and the section on graph-level, node-level, and edge-level prediction tasks, which mirrors the lecture's task taxonomy. Also note the discussion of why adjacency matrices are an awkward input for neural networks (permutation and sparsity issues) — it motivates everything that follows in the course.
- **ETA Prediction with Graph Neural Networks in Google Maps** — Austin Derrow-Pinion, Jennifer She, David Wong, Oliver Lange, et al. (DeepMind/Google) (CIKM 2021, 2021) <https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.11482>
  Proof that this week's ideas run in production for over a billion users. Focus on how a real road network is turned into a graph — the 'supersegment' construction — and on the table of ETA improvements across cities; this is the answer to 'why graphs?' in one deployed system. Note how much of the paper is about engineering realities (training stability via MetaGradients, caching for serving latency) rather than the model itself. Skim the Graph Network architecture details, which will make full sense after the GNN weeks.
- **Graph Convolutional Neural Networks for Web-Scale Recommender Systems** — Rex Ying, Ruining He, Kaifeng Chen, Pong Eksombatchai, William L. Hamilton, Jure Leskovec (KDD 2018, 2018) <https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.01973>
  The PinSage paper: the first GCN deployed on a web-scale graph (Pinterest's 3-billion-node bipartite graph of pins and boards). Read it as an example of a graph type from lecture — a bipartite graph — driving a real recommendation product, and look for how random-walk-based neighborhood sampling sidesteps ever materializing the full adjacency structure. The offline, A/B, and human evaluation setup is a good template for how industry validates graph ML. Defer the convolution math to the GNN weeks; here, the systems story is the point.

## Week 2

**Required**

- **Weisfeiler-Lehman Graph Kernels** — Nino Shervashidze, Pascal Schweitzer, Erik Jan van Leeuwen, Kurt Mehlhorn, Karsten M. Borgwardt (JMLR 2011 (vol. 12, pp. 2539-2561), 2011) <https://jmlr.org/papers/v12/shervashidze11a.html>
  This is the paper of the week: focus on Section 3, where the Weisfeiler-Lehman relabeling scheme turns each node's neighborhood into a compressed label, and make sure you can run 2-3 iterations of the algorithm by hand on a small graph (Figure 2 walks through exactly this). Understand why the WL subtree kernel is just a dot product of label-count histograms and why one iteration costs O(m), which is what made kernels scale to large graph datasets. The general framework of lifting any base kernel to a WL kernel (edge, shortest-path variants) is worth skimming; you can skip the detailed runtime proofs and most of the experimental tables. Keep this paper in mind for later weeks: the same color-refinement idea reappears as the expressiveness ceiling of message-passing GNNs.
- **Fast unfolding of communities in large networks** — Vincent D. Blondel, Jean-Loup Guillaume, Renaud Lambiotte, Etienne Lefebvre (Journal of Statistical Mechanics: Theory and Experiment 2008 (P10008), 2008) <https://arxiv.org/abs/0803.0476>
  The Louvain method in 6 pages: study the two-phase loop in Section 2 (greedy local moving of nodes to maximize modularity gain, then aggregating communities into super-nodes) and Figure 1, which shows one full pass. Work through the modularity-gain formula ΔQ so you see why each move can be evaluated in constant time using only local sums — that is the entire reason the method scales to hundreds of millions of nodes. Also note the discussion of hierarchy (each aggregation level is a coarser community structure) and the brief remark on the resolution limit, which sets up the Leiden paper in the optional list. Skip nothing; the paper is short and entirely readable.
**Optional**

- **From Louvain to Leiden: guaranteeing well-connected communities** — Vincent A. Traag, Ludo Waltman, Nees Jan van Eck (Scientific Reports 2019 (9:5233), 2019) <https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.08473>
  Read this as a critique of the required Louvain paper: its central finding is that Louvain can produce communities that are internally disconnected, and Figure 1-2 show concretely how a node move severs a community. Look at the refinement phase that Leiden adds and the guarantees it buys (connected, and eventually subset-optimal, communities). Skim the guarantee proofs; the empirical comparison of speed and quality is enough. If you ever call a library's community-detection routine in practice, this paper explains why the default is now usually Leiden, not Louvain.
- **Modularity and community structure in networks** — M. E. J. Newman (PNAS 2006 (vol. 103, pp. 8577-8582), 2006) <https://arxiv.org/abs/physics/0602124>
  This gives you the meaning of the modularity Q that Louvain greedily optimizes: read the definition of modularity as edges-within-communities minus the null-model expectation, and understand why comparing against a random degree-preserving graph matters. The main technical contribution — rewriting modularity in terms of eigenvectors of the modularity matrix and splitting communities by the sign of the leading eigenvector — is a beautiful bridge to the spectral methods you will meet later in the course. Follow the two-community derivation; you may skip the repeated-bisection details and the applications section.
- **Efficient graphlet kernels for large graph comparison** — Nino Shervashidze, S. V. N. Vishwanathan, Tobias Petri, Kurt Mehlhorn, Karsten Borgwardt (AISTATS 2009 (PMLR vol. 5), 2009) <https://proceedings.mlr.press/v5/shervashidze09a.html>
  This is the graphlet counterpart to the WL kernel: two graphs are compared by their distributions of small induced subgraphs (graphlets), which connects directly to the graphlet-degree ideas from lecture. Focus on the definition of the graphlet kernel as an inner product of normalized graphlet-count vectors, and on the two tricks that make counting feasible — sampling with a concentration bound on how many samples suffice, and exact counting for bounded-degree graphs. The proofs and the bioinformatics experiments can be skimmed; take away why exhaustive subgraph counting is expensive and how sampling sidesteps it.
- **A Survey on Graph Kernels** — Nils M. Kriege, Fredrik D. Johansson, Christopher Morris (Applied Network Science 2020 (5:6), 2020) <https://arxiv.org/abs/1903.11835>
  Use this survey as a map, not a deep read: it organizes the whole graph-kernel zoo (walk-, shortest-path-, subgraph-, and WL-based kernels) into one taxonomy, so after reading the two Shervashidze papers you can see where they sit. The most useful parts are the taxonomy figure/table and the large experimental comparison at the end, which tells you which kernels actually win on standard benchmarks (WL variants, most of the time) and when kernels beat or lose to GNNs. Skip the sections on kernels for continuous attributes and the theoretical expressivity discussion on a first pass.

## Week 3

**Required**

- **DeepWalk: Online Learning of Social Representations** — Bryan Perozzi, Rami Al-Rfou, Steven Skiena (KDD 2014, 2014) <https://arxiv.org/abs/1403.6652>
  This is the paper that imported word2vec into graph land: truncated random walks are treated as 'sentences' and nodes as 'words', so SkipGram learns node embeddings. Focus on Section 4 (the walk generation + SkipGram pipeline) and Figure 1, which shows how 2D embeddings preserve community structure in Zachary's karate club. Also note Section 3.1's observation that node visit frequencies in random walks follow a power law like words in text — that analogy is the whole justification. You can skim the hierarchical softmax details (Section 4.2.2); modern implementations use negative sampling instead.
- **node2vec: Scalable Feature Learning for Networks** — Aditya Grover, Jure Leskovec (KDD 2016, 2016) <https://arxiv.org/abs/1607.00653>
  The key idea is a single change to DeepWalk: a 2nd-order biased random walk with return parameter p and in-out parameter q that interpolates between BFS-like and DFS-like exploration. Study Figure 2 and the search-bias equations in Section 3.2 until you can predict what walks look like for extreme p and q, and connect that to homophily vs. structural equivalence (Figure 1 and the Les Miserables case study in Figure 3). Pay attention to how link prediction is set up via edge features from node-embedding binary operators (Table 1) — this evaluation recipe is still standard. Skim the sensitivity/perturbation experiments.
**Optional**

- **PageRank beyond the Web** — David F. Gleich (SIAM Review 2015, 2015) <https://arxiv.org/abs/1407.5107>
  Read this instead of the 1999 Stanford tech report for the PageRank half of the week: Section 2 gives the cleanest modern statement of the random-surfer model, the teleportation parameter alpha, and the linear-system vs. eigenvector formulations you saw in lecture. Then browse the applications catalog (biology, bibliometrics, recommendation, road networks) to see PageRank as a general graph centrality, not a web ranking hack. Skip the numerical-methods and sensitivity sections — just take away why the computation converges and what alpha controls.
- **LINE: Large-scale Information Network Embedding** — Jian Tang, Meng Qu, Mingzhe Wang, Ming Zhang, Jun Yan, Qiaozhu Mei (WWW 2015, 2015) <https://arxiv.org/abs/1503.03578>
  LINE drops random walks entirely and instead defines two explicit objectives: first-order proximity (connected nodes should be close) and second-order proximity (nodes with similar neighborhoods should be close) — see Section 4 and Figure 1's illustration of why both matter. Note the edge-sampling trick with negative sampling that makes training scale to hundred-million-edge graphs. Read it as a design alternative to DeepWalk: same encoder-decoder skeleton, different similarity definition. Skim the experiments; the objective definitions are the payoff.
- **Network Embedding as Matrix Factorization: Unifying DeepWalk, LINE, PTE, and node2vec** — Jiezhong Qiu, Yuxiao Dong, Hao Ma, Jian Li, Kuansan Wang, Jie Tang (WSDM 2018, 2018) <https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.02971>
  This is the matrix-factorization equivalence result from lecture, in full: DeepWalk, LINE, PTE, and node2vec with negative sampling are all implicitly factorizing closed-form matrices built from the graph's normalized adjacency/Laplacian. Table 1 is the paper in one page — study it, and read Section 2 far enough to see how the DeepWalk matrix (a log of averaged transition-matrix powers) is derived; note that LINE falls out as DeepWalk with window T=1. The proofs and the spectral bounds are heavier theory you can safely skip; the NetMF algorithm and its wins over DeepWalk (Section 4) show the equivalence is practically useful, not just elegant.
- **Representation Learning on Graphs: Methods and Applications** — William L. Hamilton, Rex Ying, Jure Leskovec (IEEE Data Engineering Bulletin 2017, 2017) <https://arxiv.org/abs/1709.05584>
  Read Section 2 for the encoder-decoder framework the course uses: every shallow embedding method is just an embedding-lookup encoder plus a pairwise decoder and a similarity definition — the table mapping DeepWalk, node2vec, LINE, and factorization methods into this template is the most useful summary you will find. Then read Section 2.3's list of shallow-embedding limitations (no parameter sharing, no node features, transductive-only) closely; it is the exact motivation for the GNNs coming later in the course. Skip Sections 3-4 on GNNs and subgraph embeddings for now and return to them in later weeks.

## Week 4

**Required**

- **Translating Embeddings for Modeling Multi-relational Data** — Antoine Bordes, Nicolas Usunier, Alberto Garcia-Duran, Jason Weston, Oksana Yakhnenko (NeurIPS 2013, 2013) <https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/2013/hash/1cecc7a77928ca8133fa24680a88d2f9-Abstract.html>
  This is TransE, the paper that started modern KG embeddings: a relation is just a translation vector, so a true triple (h, r, t) should satisfy h + r ≈ t. Focus on Section 2 — the scoring function and the margin-based ranking loss with corrupted (negatively sampled) triples — and on Table 3, where mean rank and Hits@10 are defined the way the whole field still evaluates. Also note the 'filtered' vs 'raw' evaluation distinction, which you will reuse all semester. You can skim the related-work comparison to older tensor models (SE, RESCAL) on a first pass.
- **RotatE: Knowledge Graph Embedding by Relational Rotation in Complex Space** — Zhiqing Sun, Zhi-Hong Deng, Jian-Yun Nie, Jian Tang (ICLR 2019, 2019) <https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.10197>
  The strongest direct descendant of TransE: relations become rotations in complex space, t = h ∘ r with |r_i| = 1. Read Table 1 and Section 3 carefully — they show which relation patterns (symmetry, antisymmetry, inversion, composition) each model (TransE, DistMult, ComplEx, RotatE) can and cannot express, which is the best one-page summary of this whole model family. Then study Section 3.3 on self-adversarial negative sampling; it is a training trick that matters as much as the model itself. The extensive ablation tables at the end can be skimmed.
**Optional**

- **Embedding Entities and Relations for Learning and Inference in Knowledge Bases** — Bishan Yang, Wen-tau Yih, Xiaodong He, Jianfeng Gao, Li Deng (ICLR 2015, 2015) <https://arxiv.org/abs/1412.6575>
  This is the DistMult paper: score a triple with a simple bilinear product using a diagonal relation matrix. Read Section 2 to see how translation-based (TransE-style) and bilinear (RESCAL-style) models fit into one unified framework — that framing alone is worth the read. Note DistMult's built-in flaw: the diagonal product is symmetric, so it cannot distinguish (h, r, t) from (t, r, h); this motivates ComplEx. Section 5 on mining Horn rules from embeddings is interesting but safely skippable.
- **Complex Embeddings for Simple Link Prediction** — Théo Trouillon, Johannes Welbl, Sebastian Riedel, Éric Gaussier, Guillaume Bouchard (ICML 2016, 2016) <https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.06357>
  ComplEx fixes DistMult's symmetry problem by moving embeddings into complex space, where the Hermitian dot product is no longer symmetric — so antisymmetric relations become learnable at no extra cost. The key idea is in Sections 3-4: take the real part of a trilinear complex product. The linear-algebra background (unitary diagonalization of normal matrices) is the heaviest part; it is fine to accept the theorem and focus on the scoring function and the experiments on symmetric vs antisymmetric synthetic relations.
- **Observed versus Latent Features for Knowledge Base and Text Inference** — Kristina Toutanova, Danqi Chen (CVSC Workshop at ACL-IJCNLP 2015, 2015) <https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/W15-4007>
  A short workshop paper with outsized impact: it showed that the standard FB15k benchmark leaks — many test triples are just inverses of training triples — so a trivial rule-based model looks competitive with embeddings. This is why everyone now evaluates on FB15k-237, the dataset introduced here. Read it as a lesson in benchmark hygiene: look for how the inverse-relation leakage is diagnosed and how the 237-relation subset is constructed. It is only a few pages; read all of it.
- **A Re-evaluation of Knowledge Graph Completion Methods** — Zhiqing Sun, Shikhar Vashishth, Soumya Sanyal, Partha Talukdar, Yiming Yang (ACL 2020, 2020) <https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.03903>
  A short, sharp critique of how MRR and Hits@K are computed in practice: several neural KG completion models assigned identical scores to many candidate triples, and the evaluation code's tie-breaking (placing the true triple first) inflated their reported numbers. Focus on the analysis of score ties and the proposed random tie-breaking protocol — it will make you permanently suspicious of leaderboard numbers. A good pairing with Toutanova & Chen: one paper is about broken datasets, this one is about broken evaluation code.

## Week 5

**Required**

- **Query2box: Reasoning over Knowledge Graphs in Vector Space using Box Embeddings** — Hongyu Ren, Weihua Hu, Jure Leskovec (ICLR 2020, 2020) <https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.05969>
  This is the week's core method: answering complex logical queries (with conjunctions, disjunctions, and existential quantifiers) by embedding queries as boxes (axis-aligned hyper-rectangles) so that answer entities fall inside the box. Focus on why single-point embeddings fail for queries with many answers, how projection and intersection operators act on boxes, and how the DNF trick reduces any EPFO query to a union of conjunctive queries. Study Figures 1-2 (the query computation graph and box operations) carefully; you can skim the theoretical remarks and hyperparameter details. Make sure you can trace one 2-hop query end to end through the model.
- **From Local to Global: A Graph RAG Approach to Query-Focused Summarization** — Darren Edge, Ha Trinh, Newman Cheng, Joshua Bradley, Alex Chao, Apurva Mody, Steven Truitt, Dasha Metropolitansky, Robert Osazuwa Ness, Jonathan Larson (arXiv preprint, 2024) <https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.16130>
  This is the Microsoft GraphRAG paper that connects knowledge graphs to LLM retrieval: an LLM extracts an entity graph from a corpus, community detection (Leiden) partitions it hierarchically, and pre-computed community summaries are map-reduced to answer global 'sensemaking' questions that vanilla vector RAG cannot. Focus on the pipeline figure and on why naive top-k chunk retrieval fails for questions about a whole corpus. Note how they evaluate with an LLM-as-judge on comprehensiveness and diversity, and think critically about the weaknesses of that evaluation. Skim the prompt-engineering appendix details.
**Optional**

- **Beta Embeddings for Multi-Hop Logical Reasoning in Knowledge Graphs** — Hongyu Ren, Jure Leskovec (NeurIPS 2020, 2020) <https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.11465>
  The direct sequel to Query2box by the same group: queries and entities become Beta distributions, which finally makes negation ('NOT') representable — something boxes cannot do (the complement of a box is not a box). Read it as a case study in how a representation's geometry limits which logical operators it supports; the probabilistic machinery (KL-divergence distance, De Morgan's laws for unions) is heavier, so focus on Figure 1 and the negation operator and skim the derivations. Compare its handling of unions with Query2box's DNF rewriting.
- **A Re-evaluation of Knowledge Graph Completion Methods** — Zhiqing Sun, Shikhar Vashishth, Soumya Sanyal, Partha Talukdar, Yiming Yang (ACL 2020, 2020) <https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.03903>
  A short, sharp critique showing that several published KG-completion results were inflated by how ties in ranking scores were broken during evaluation — some neural models assign identical scores to many candidates and 'win' by being placed first among ties. Look for the definition of the random tie-breaking protocol they propose and the before/after tables on FB15k-237 and WN18RR. This is the week's lesson on evaluation hygiene: MRR and Hits@k numbers are only comparable if the protocol is identical. Very readable; read it in one sitting.
- **Unifying Large Language Models and Knowledge Graphs: A Roadmap** — Shirui Pan, Linhao Luo, Yufei Wang, Chen Chen, Jiapu Wang, Xindong Wu (IEEE TKDE 2024, 2024) <https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.08302>
  A survey that organizes the KG+LLM space into three frameworks: KG-enhanced LLMs, LLM-augmented KGs, and synergized systems where the two cooperate. Do not read it linearly — study the roadmap figure and the taxonomy tables, then dip into the one or two subsections closest to your project interests (e.g., KG-augmented retrieval or LLM-based KG construction). Use it to place GraphRAG from the required reading into the broader landscape, and to mine its bibliography for follow-up papers.
- **Towards Foundation Models for Knowledge Graph Reasoning** — Mikhail Galkin, Xinyu Yuan, Hesham Mostafa, Jian Tang, Zhaocheng Zhu (ICLR 2024, 2024) <https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.04562>
  ULTRA is the 'foundation model' answer to a fundamental limitation of everything else this week: classic KG embeddings learn a fixed vocabulary of entities and relations, so they cannot transfer to a new graph. ULTRA instead learns from a graph of relation-to-relation interactions, letting one pretrained model do zero-shot link prediction on 50+ unseen KGs, often beating models trained on each graph. Focus on Section 3 (how relative relation representations are built) and the transfer-learning results table; skim the NBFNet backbone details. Ask yourself what 'inductive' means here versus in Query2box.

## Week 6

**Required**

- **Semi-Supervised Classification with Graph Convolutional Networks** — Thomas N. Kipf, Max Welling (ICLR 2017, 2017) <https://arxiv.org/abs/1609.02907>
  This is the paper of the week: the GCN layer H' = sigma(D^{-1/2} A D^{-1/2} H W) that most graph ML libraries still ship as the default. Work through Section 2 line by line — it derives this layer as a first-order truncation of spectral (Chebyshev) filters, which is exactly the spectral-to-spatial story from lecture; make sure you can explain the renormalization trick (adding self-loops) and why it stabilizes training. Then study the semi-supervised setup in Section 3 and Table 2: only a few labeled nodes per class, with the graph structure spreading label information. You can skim the Weisfeiler-Lehman appendix on first read, but do convince yourself the layer is permutation equivariant by checking what happens when you permute A and H together.
- **Neural Message Passing for Quantum Chemistry** — Justin Gilmer, Samuel S. Schoenholz, Patrick F. Riley, Oriol Vinyals, George E. Dahl (ICML 2017, 2017) <https://arxiv.org/abs/1704.01212>
  Read this as the follow-up that unifies the field: Section 2 defines the Message Passing Neural Network (MPNN) abstraction — message, update, and readout functions — and shows that GCN, Gated Graph NNs, and several chemistry models are all special cases; that three-function template is the single most reusable idea you will take from this week. Note how the readout must be a permutation-invariant set function (e.g., a sum) so the whole graph-level model is invariant even though the per-node layers are equivariant. The quantum-chemistry specifics (QM9 targets, edge networks, the set2set readout) are worth a lighter read for how the abstraction is instantiated; you can skip the detailed chemistry accuracy discussion in the results.
**Optional**

- **Convolutional Neural Networks on Graphs with Fast Localized Spectral Filtering** — Michaël Defferrard, Xavier Bresson, Pierre Vandergheynst (NeurIPS 2016, 2016) <https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.09375>
  ChebNet is the missing middle step in the spectral-to-spatial derivation: it replaces exact eigendecomposition-based spectral filters with K-th order Chebyshev polynomials of the Laplacian, making filters K-hop localized and O(edges) to evaluate. Focus on Section 2 (the polynomial parametrization and why it avoids computing eigenvectors) and the K-locality argument; the GCN you read this week is literally this model with K=1 plus a renormalization. The graph coarsening/pooling machinery in Section 2.3 is dated — skim it.
- **Simplifying Graph Convolutional Networks** — Felix Wu, Tianyi Zhang, Amauri Holanda de Souza Jr., Christopher Fifty, Tao Yu, Kilian Q. Weinberger (ICML 2019, 2019) <https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.07153>
  Read this as a critique of the GCN paper: the authors strip out all nonlinearities and collapse the weight matrices, leaving logistic regression on S^K X (K propagation steps of the normalized adjacency), and still match GCN on the standard citation benchmarks. The key insight is Section 3's spectral analysis showing that GCN-style propagation acts as a fixed low-pass filter — the feature smoothing, not the deep nonlinear architecture, does most of the work. Ask yourself what this says about the benchmarks as much as about the model. Short and easy after you have read Kipf & Welling.
- **Spectral Networks and Locally Connected Networks on Graphs** — Joan Bruna, Wojciech Zaremba, Arthur Szlam, Yann LeCun (ICLR 2014, 2014) <https://arxiv.org/abs/1312.6203>
  This is where the spectral story begins: convolution on a graph is defined as multiplication in the graph Laplacian's eigenbasis, in direct analogy to the Fourier convolution theorem. Read Section 3 for the spectral construction and note its two costs — O(n^2) filter application via dense eigenvectors and no guaranteed spatial localization — because these are exactly the problems ChebNet and GCN later fix. This is the heaviest math of the week; it is fine to read for the ideas and skip the harmonic-analysis details and experiments.
- **Relational inductive biases, deep learning, and graph networks** — Peter W. Battaglia, Jessica B. Hamrick, Victor Bapst, et al. (arXiv preprint, 2018) <https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.01261>
  A position-paper-plus-survey that frames this week's third theme: permutation equivariance/invariance as the relational inductive bias that makes GNNs the graph analogue of CNNs' translation equivariance. Read Sections 1-2 for the inductive-bias argument (Table 1 comparing architectures is the key figure) and Section 3.2 for the Graph Network block, which generalizes the MPNN template you saw in Gilmer et al. with edge, node, and global update functions. It is long — treat it as a browsing read and skip the discussion of the accompanying software library.

## Week 7

**Required**

- **Inductive Representation Learning on Large Graphs** — William L. Hamilton, Rex Ying, Jure Leskovec (NeurIPS 2017, 2017) <https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.02216>
  This is GraphSAGE, the week's core method: instead of learning a fixed embedding per node, it learns aggregator functions that sample and combine a node's neighborhood features, which is what makes it inductive (it can embed nodes never seen in training). Work through Algorithm 1 line by line and make sure you can explain why sampling a fixed-size neighborhood bounds the cost of each minibatch. Compare the aggregator variants (mean, LSTM, pooling) in Section 3.3 and connect them to the aggregation-choice discussion from lecture. You can skim Section 3.4 and the theoretical analysis on expressing clustering coefficients; the empirical sections on Reddit and PPI matter more for this week.
- **Graph Attention Networks** — Petar Veličković, Guillem Cucurull, Arantxa Casanova, Adriana Romero, Pietro Liò, Yoshua Bengio (ICLR 2018, 2018) <https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.10903>
  GAT replaces fixed aggregators with learned attention: each node computes a weight for every neighbor, so aggregation becomes a weighted mean whose weights depend on the features themselves. Focus on Section 2.1 and Figure 1 — derive the attention coefficients by hand (linear map, concatenation, LeakyReLU, softmax over the neighborhood) and note how multi-head attention plays the role that channels play in CNNs. Ask yourself what GAT can express that GraphSAGE's mean aggregator cannot, and what it costs. The comparison to related work in Section 2.2 is worth reading; the transductive benchmark details can be skimmed.
**Optional**

- **How Powerful are Graph Neural Networks?** — Keyulu Xu, Weihua Hu, Jure Leskovec, Stefanie Jegelka (ICLR 2019, 2019) <https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.00826>
  This is the GIN paper — we return to it in full in week 9, so this week read it only as an answer to "which aggregator should I pick?". Look at Figures 2 and 3 for concrete neighborhoods that mean and max aggregators cannot tell apart but sum can, and read Section 4.1 for why summation preserves the multiset of neighbor features. Skip the Weisfeiler-Lehman proofs and Lemmas for now; just take away the injectivity intuition behind the sum aggregator and the MLP-plus-epsilon update in Equation 4.1.
- **Design Space for Graph Neural Networks** — Jiaxuan You, Rex Ying, Jure Leskovec (NeurIPS 2020, 2020) <https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.08843>
  This paper turns this week's grab-bag of design choices — aggregation, skip connections, batch normalization, dropout, depth, layer ordering — into a systematic 315,000-configuration study, and it is the empirical backbone of the lecture. Read Section 3 for how the design space is defined, then spend your time on Figure 4 and the ranking analysis: which choices matter consistently (e.g., skip connections, sum aggregation) and which are task-dependent. You do not need the details of the task-similarity metric or GraphGym internals; treat those sections as reference material for when you run your own experiments.
- **How Attentive are Graph Attention Networks?** — Shaked Brody, Uri Alon, Eran Yahav (ICLR 2022, 2022) <https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.14491>
  A sharp critique of the required GAT paper: it shows GAT computes only "static" attention — the ranking of neighbors is the same for every query node — and fixes it (GATv2) by simply reordering the linear map and the nonlinearity in the attention score. Read Section 3 for the static-vs-dynamic definitions and the dictionary-lookup toy problem GAT provably cannot solve, and compare Equations for GAT and GATv2 side by side; the fix is one line. This is a great case study in reading a famous architecture critically. Skim the proofs and the robustness experiments.
- **DropEdge: Towards Deep Graph Convolutional Networks on Node Classification** — Yu Rong, Wenbing Huang, Tingyang Xu, Junzhou Huang (ICLR 2020, 2020) <https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.10903>
  This covers the regularization corner of the design space: randomly deleting a fraction of edges each training epoch acts both as data augmentation (against over-fitting) and as a brake on over-smoothing (the tendency of deep GNNs to make all node representations converge). Read Sections 1, 3.1, and 4.1-4.2 for the method and its interaction with backbones like GraphSAGE, ResGCN, and JKNet, and note how it differs from feature dropout. The over-smoothing theory in Section 3.2 can be skimmed on a first pass — take the theorem statements on faith and focus on the ablations.

## Week 9

**Required**

- **How Powerful are Graph Neural Networks?** — Keyulu Xu, Weihua Hu, Jure Leskovec, Stefanie Jegelka (ICLR 2019, 2019) <https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.00826>
  This is the paper that connects message-passing GNNs to the Weisfeiler-Lehman (WL) graph isomorphism test and derives GIN as the maximally expressive architecture in that class. Focus on Theorem 3 (no message-passing GNN can beat 1-WL) and Corollary 6/Theorem 4, and make sure you can explain in your own words why the aggregator must be injective over multisets — Figure 2's ranking of sum vs. mean vs. max captures the whole argument in one picture. Also study the simple graph pairs in Figure 3 that mean/max pooling cannot distinguish; reproducing that reasoning by hand is the best self-test. You can skim the proofs in the appendix, but do not skip Section 5, where the theory turns into the concrete GIN update equation you will see in libraries.
- **On the Bottleneck of Graph Neural Networks and its Practical Implications** — Uri Alon, Eran Yahav (ICLR 2021, 2021) <https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.05205>
  This paper names and demonstrates over-squashing: information from an exponentially growing receptive field gets compressed through fixed-size node vectors, so long-range dependencies fail. Start with Figure 1 comparing the bottleneck in GNNs to the classic seq2seq bottleneck in RNNs, then work through the synthetic NeighborsMatch (Tree-NeighborsMatch) problem, which cleanly isolates the effect from other confounders like oversmoothing. Pay attention to the strikingly simple fix — making the last layer fully adjacent — and how much it helps on real chemistry benchmarks; that is a lesson in how a cheap diagnostic experiment can expose a structural flaw in a whole model family. Contrast this failure mode with oversmoothing from the lecture: they are related but distinct, and being able to articulate the difference is the goal of this week.
**Optional**

- **Weisfeiler and Leman Go Neural: Higher-order Graph Neural Networks** — Christopher Morris, Martin Ritzert, Matthias Fey, William L. Hamilton, Jan Eric Lenssen, Gaurav Rattan, Martin Grohe (AAAI 2019, 2019) <https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.02244>
  The independent companion to the GIN paper: it proves the same 1-WL upper bound on message-passing GNNs and then goes further, lifting message passing to k-tuples of nodes (k-GNNs) to break past the 1-WL ceiling. Read Sections 3-4 for the k-WL hierarchy and the hierarchical 1-2-3-GNN construction, and note the computational cost that comes with higher-order expressiveness — this trade-off is why higher-order GNNs are not the default in practice. The proofs are heavier than Xu et al.; skim them and focus on what k-GNNs can distinguish that GIN cannot.
- **Deeper Insights into Graph Convolutional Networks for Semi-Supervised Learning** — Qimai Li, Zhichao Han, Xiao-Ming Wu (AAAI 2018, 2018) <https://arxiv.org/abs/1801.07606>
  The paper that first identified oversmoothing: GCN's propagation is a special form of Laplacian smoothing, so stacking many layers drives node features within a connected component toward the same value. Section 3 is the core — the reinterpretation of the GCN layer as Laplacian smoothing and the theorem on repeated smoothing converging to indistinguishable features; the visualization of Zachary's karate club embeddings across layers makes it concrete. The co-training/self-training remedies in the second half are dated, so skim them; the diagnosis, not the cure, is why this paper is still cited.
- **Understanding over-squashing and bottlenecks on graphs via curvature** — Jake Topping, Francesco Di Giovanni, Benjamin Paul Chamberlain, Xiaowen Dong, Michael M. Bronstein (ICLR 2022, 2022) <https://arxiv.org/abs/2111.14522>
  The theoretical follow-up to Alon & Yahav: it formalizes over-squashing via the Jacobian of node representations and shows that negatively curved edges (a discrete Ricci curvature notion) are where the bottleneck lives. This is the most mathematically demanding paper of the week — read for the intuition that curvature measures how tree-like/expanding a neighborhood is, look at the Jacobian sensitivity bound in Section 3, and understand the idea of the SDRF rewiring algorithm (surgically add edges around negatively curved ones) without following every proof. It received an outstanding-paper honorable mention at ICLR 2022, and it is a nice example of differential-geometry ideas entering graph ML.
- **Graph Neural Networks Exponentially Lose Expressive Power for Node Classification** — Kenta Oono, Taiji Suzuki (ICLR 2020, 2020) <https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.10947>
  A rigorous treatment of oversmoothing: as depth grows, GCN representations (with ReLU and standard normalization) converge exponentially fast toward a low-information subspace determined by connected components and node degrees, with speed governed by the graph's spectral gap. Read the setup and the statement of the main theorem for the 'distance to the invariant subspace shrinks like lambda^L' picture; the dynamical-systems proof machinery is optional. Pair it with Li et al. — same phenomenon, but here nonlinearity is handled and the convergence rate is explicit.

## Week 10

**Required**

- **Modeling Relational Data with Graph Convolutional Networks** — Michael Schlichtkrull, Thomas N. Kipf, Peter Bloem, Rianne van den Berg, Ivan Titov, Max Welling (ESWC 2018, 2018) <https://arxiv.org/abs/1703.06103>
  This is the paper that extends GCN message passing to multi-relational graphs: study Section 2 and the R-GCN update equation, where each relation type (and each direction) gets its own weight matrix summed over per-relation neighborhoods. Pay close attention to the basis and block-diagonal decompositions -- they exist because naive per-relation weights blow up parameter counts on real KGs, and this trick reappears in many later models. Then look at the encoder-decoder setup for link prediction (R-GCN encoder + DistMult scorer), which is the template for GNNs-on-KGs used throughout this week. You can skim the per-dataset experimental details.
- **Heterogeneous Graph Transformer** — Ziniu Hu, Yuxiao Dong, Kuansan Wang, Yizhou Sun (WWW 2020, 2020) <https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.01332>
  Read this as the modern follow-up to R-GCN: instead of one weight matrix per relation, HGT parameterizes transformer-style attention by the meta-relation triple (source type, edge type, target type), so parameters are shared across relations while staying type-aware. Focus on Section 4 and the architecture figure showing Heterogeneous Mutual Attention, Message Passing, and Target-Specific Aggregation, and ask yourself how this avoids the hand-designed meta-paths that earlier models (like HAN) require. The relative temporal encoding and HGSampling sections are about scaling to a 179M-node academic graph -- skim them unless large-scale training interests you.
**Optional**

- **Heterogeneous Graph Attention Network** — Xiao Wang, Houye Ji, Chuan Shi, Bai Wang, Peng Cui, Philip S. Yu, Yanfang Ye (WWW 2019, 2019) <https://arxiv.org/abs/1903.07293>
  HAN is the meta-path-based alternative to HGT: node-level attention aggregates over meta-path-defined neighbors, then semantic-level attention weighs the meta-paths themselves. Focus on the hierarchical attention figure and make sure you can explain what a meta-path (e.g., author-paper-author) buys you and what it costs (a human must choose them). Reading this before or alongside HGT makes HGT's 'no hand-designed meta-paths' pitch much clearer; the attention-visualization experiments are a nice interpretability bonus.
- **Are we really making much progress? Revisiting, benchmarking, and refining heterogeneous graph neural networks** — Qingsong Lv, Ming Ding, Qiang Liu, Yuxiang Chen, Wenzheng Feng, Siming He, Chang Zhou, Jianguo Jiang, Yuxiao Dong, Jie Tang (KDD 2021, 2021) <https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.14936>
  A healthy dose of skepticism after HAN and HGT: the authors reproduce 12 heterogeneous GNNs and show that a properly tuned plain GAT matches or beats most of them. Look for where the reported gains actually came from (feature preprocessing, data splits, tuning) and at Simple-HGN, which adds just learnable edge-type embeddings to GAT. Read this to learn how to evaluate architecture claims as a future ML engineer; skip the exhaustive per-dataset tables and keep the methodology lessons.
- **Composition-based Multi-Relational Graph Convolutional Networks** — Shikhar Vashishth, Soumya Sanyal, Vikram Nitin, Partha Talukdar (ICLR 2020, 2020) <https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.03082>
  CompGCN answers R-GCN's parameter-explosion problem differently: embed relations as vectors and combine them with entity embeddings via composition operators borrowed from KG embedding models (subtraction from TransE, multiplication from DistMult, circular correlation from HolE). Focus on the composition operation in the update rule and on the table showing how R-GCN and other multi-relational GCNs fall out as special cases. The notation is denser than the required papers, so treat it as a theory-leaning read connecting Week 10 back to KG embedding methods.
- **Towards Foundation Models for Knowledge Graph Reasoning** — Mikhail Galkin, Xinyu Yuan, Hesham Mostafa, Jian Tang, Zhaocheng Zhu (ICLR 2024, 2024) <https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.04562>
  ULTRA is the modern bridge from this week's relational GNNs to foundation models: one pre-trained model does zero-shot link prediction on any KG with any relation vocabulary. The key idea to extract is the graph-of-relations -- relations get representations from how they interact (shared head/tail entities) rather than from a fixed learned vocabulary, which is what makes transfer possible. Study the method figure and the zero-shot vs. supervised comparison across 57 KGs; you can skip the NBFNet implementation details it builds on.

## Week 11

**Required**

- **Cluster-GCN: An Efficient Algorithm for Training Deep and Large Graph Convolutional Networks** — Wei-Lin Chiang, Xuanqing Liu, Si Si, Yang Li, Samy Bengio, Cho-Jui Hsieh (KDD 2019, 2019) <https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.07953>
  Start with Section 2's diagnosis of why full-batch GCN training blows up: the neighborhood-expansion problem, where the receptive field grows exponentially with depth. The key idea is to partition the graph with METIS into dense clusters and use each cluster (or a random union of a few clusters, the 'stochastic multiple partitions' trick that recovers between-cluster edges) as a mini-batch, so memory cost is linear in batch size regardless of depth. Study the complexity comparison table against GraphSAGE/VR-GCN and the embedding-utilization argument; you can safely skip the internals of the METIS algorithm itself and skim the diagonal-enhancement trick for very deep GCNs.
- **GraphSAINT: Graph Sampling Based Inductive Learning Method** — Hanqing Zeng, Hongkuan Zhou, Ajitesh Srivastava, Rajgopal Kannan, Viktor Prasanna (ICLR 2020, 2020) <https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.04931>
  Read this as the conceptual inversion of layer-wise sampling: instead of sampling neighbors at each layer, GraphSAINT samples a small subgraph up front and runs a full GCN on it, so every layer sees the same well-connected node set. The heart of the paper is Section 3: the loss and aggregation normalization coefficients that make the mini-batch estimator unbiased, and the edge/random-walk samplers designed to reduce its variance. Understand the statements of the unbiasedness and variance results and why the edge sampler prefers edges connecting low-degree nodes, but feel free to skip the full proofs; finish with the accuracy-vs-speed comparisons against GraphSAGE, FastGCN, and Cluster-GCN in the experiments.
**Optional**

- **Inductive Representation Learning on Large Graphs** — William L. Hamilton, Rex Ying, Jure Leskovec (NeurIPS 2017, 2017) <https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.02216>
  This is where neighbor sampling started, so revisit it through a scaling lens rather than as an architecture paper. Focus on Algorithm 1 and the fixed-size uniform neighbor sampling in Section 3, and work out why the per-node cost still grows as the product of sample sizes across layers — that neighborhood explosion is exactly what Cluster-GCN and GraphSAINT are fixing. The aggregator variants (mean/LSTM/pool) and the unsupervised loss can be skimmed if you covered them earlier in the course.
- **Simplifying Graph Convolutional Networks** — Felix Wu, Tianyi Zhang, Amauri Holanda de Souza Jr., Christopher Fifty, Tao Yu, Kilian Q. Weinberger (ICML 2019, 2019) <https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.07153>
  SGC takes the opposite route to scaling: instead of sampling, it removes the nonlinearities so a K-layer GCN collapses into one precomputable feature transform S^K X followed by logistic regression. The key things to extract are the derivation of that collapse in Section 2 and the spectral interpretation of S^K as a fixed low-pass filter in Section 3. Notice that S^K X is computed once, without gradients, which makes training embarrassingly cheap — then check the results tables to see how little accuracy this costs on citation and Reddit benchmarks; the spectral-analysis details can be skimmed on a first pass.
- **SIGN: Scalable Inception Graph Neural Networks** — Fabrizio Frasca, Emanuele Rossi, Davide Eynard, Ben Chamberlain, Michael Bronstein, Federico Monti (arXiv preprint (ICML 2020 GRL+ workshop), 2020) <https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.11198>
  Read SIGN as the generalization of SGC: precompute several diffusion operators of different sizes and types (adjacency powers, PPR, triangle-based), concatenate their outputs inception-style, and train only a simple MLP on top — no sampling anywhere. Focus on the architecture equation and the argument for why moving all graph operations into a one-off preprocessing step makes both training and inference fast and mini-batch-friendly. The ogbn-papers100M experiment (110M+ nodes) is the payoff: compare its training/inference-time tables against Cluster-GCN and GraphSAINT to see when precomputation beats sampling.
- **Open Graph Benchmark: Datasets for Machine Learning on Graphs** — Weihua Hu, Matthias Fey, Marinka Zitnik, Yuxiao Dong, Hongyu Ren, Bowen Liu, Michele Catasta, Jure Leskovec (NeurIPS 2020, 2020) <https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.00687>
  Skim rather than deep-read this one: it defines the datasets (ogbn-products, ogbn-papers100M, and friends) and the standardized splits on which every scaling method this week is judged. Pay attention to why the authors argue small benchmarks like Cora mislead — realistic scale and non-random, out-of-distribution splits change which methods win. Focus on the node-property-prediction section and the discussion of scalability challenges; the molecular and knowledge-graph datasets are useful background for other weeks but optional here.

## Week 12

**Required**

- **Link Prediction Based on Graph Neural Networks** — Muhan Zhang, Yixin Chen (NeurIPS 2018, 2018) <https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.09691>
  This is the SEAL paper: instead of hand-crafting heuristics like common neighbors or Katz, it extracts a small subgraph around each candidate link and lets a GNN learn the heuristic. Focus on the gamma-decaying heuristic theorem's message (Section 3): most classical heuristics can be well approximated from small enclosing subgraphs, which justifies the whole approach — read the statement and intuition, skip the proof details. Pay close attention to the node labeling scheme (Double-Radius Node Labeling), since marking the two target nodes is what lets the GNN know which link it is predicting; this detail is the one students most often miss and it reappears in later theory.
- **GraphRNN: Generating Realistic Graphs with Deep Auto-regressive Models** — Jiaxuan You, Rex Ying, Xiang Ren, William L. Hamilton, Jure Leskovec (ICML 2018, 2018) <https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.08773>
  The core idea: turn graph generation into a sequence problem — a graph-level RNN adds nodes one at a time while an edge-level RNN decides which previous nodes each new node connects to. Study Figure 1 and the BFS node-ordering trick, which is what tames the factorially many node orderings and bounds how far back edges can reach. Also read Section 4.3 on MMD-based evaluation of degree/clustering/orbit distributions, because 'how do we even evaluate generated graphs?' is a question that recurs with the diffusion models later in the week. You can skim the scalability details of the model variants.
**Optional**

- **Variational Graph Auto-Encoders** — Thomas N. Kipf, Max Welling (NeurIPS 2016 Bayesian Deep Learning Workshop, 2016) <https://arxiv.org/abs/1611.07308>
  A 3-page workshop paper you can read in one sitting: a GCN encoder produces latent node embeddings and a simple inner-product decoder reconstructs the adjacency matrix, trained as a VAE. Look at how the ELBO from your generative-models background transfers almost unchanged to graphs, and note that link prediction here is just scoring reconstructed edges — contrast this whole-graph embedding view with SEAL's subgraph view. Also compare the non-probabilistic GAE baseline with the variational version in Table 1 and ask yourself how much the 'V' actually buys.
- **DiGress: Discrete Denoising diffusion for graph generation** — Clement Vignac, Igor Krawczuk, Antoine Siraudin, Bohan Wang, Volkan Cevher, Pascal Frossard (ICLR 2023, 2023) <https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.14734>
  The lecture's denoising-diffusion anchor: DiGress corrupts graphs with a discrete Markov process that flips node and edge categories, and a graph transformer learns to undo the noise. Focus on why discrete diffusion fits graphs better than adding Gaussian noise to an adjacency matrix (Sections 1 and 3), and on the marginal-preserving noise model. Contrast the one-shot iterative denoising with GraphRNN's sequential generation — this is the key conceptual shift of the week. Skip the spectral-feature augmentation and conditioning details on a first pass.
- **Diffusion-based Graph Generative Methods** — Hongyang Chen, Can Xu, Lingyu Zheng, Qiang Zhang, Xuemin Lin (IEEE TKDE 2024) <https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.15617>
  Use this survey as a map, not a deep read: it organizes graph diffusion work into the three paradigms (DDPM-style, score-based, SDE-based) and catalogs where methods like DiGress sit among them. Read the taxonomy and the tables to see how the field evolved after GraphRNN, and skim the application sections (molecules, proteins) for motivation. Skip the SDE mathematics unless you have had a stochastic-processes course; the categorization is what you need for the exam-level picture.
- **Labeling Trick: A Theory of Using Graph Neural Networks for Multi-Node Representation Learning** — Muhan Zhang, Pan Li, Yinglong Xia, Kai Wang, Long Jin (NeurIPS 2021, 2021) <https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.16103>
  The theory sequel to SEAL, for the mathematically inclined: it explains formally why naively aggregating two node embeddings (as VGAE's decoder does) cannot distinguish links that a labeled-subgraph method can, e.g. nodes that are automorphic but not linked the same way. Read the motivating failure example in Figure 1 and the definition of a labeling trick; the main theorem says GNNs plus labeling can learn most-expressive structural representations of node sets. This is the heaviest reading of the week — it is enough to take away why SEAL's node labeling is not a cosmetic detail.

## Week 13

**Required**

- **Do Transformers Really Perform Bad for Graph Representation?** — Chengxuan Ying, Tianle Cai, Shengjie Luo, Shuxin Zheng, Guolin Ke, Di He, Yanming Shen, Tie-Yan Liu (NeurIPS 2021, 2021) <https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.05234>
  This is the Graphormer paper — the week's core method. Focus on Section 3: the three structural encodings (centrality encoding added to node features, spatial encoding via shortest-path distance, and edge encoding) are all injected as simple bias terms in the attention score, so a vanilla Transformer 'sees' graph structure without any message passing. Study Figure 1 and the attention-bias equation until you can explain why attention over all node pairs plus these biases subsumes neighborhood aggregation; the expressiveness facts in Section 3.3 (Graphormer can express mean/sum GNN aggregation) are worth reading, but you can skip the proofs in the appendix and the OGB-LSC leaderboard engineering details.
- **Recipe for a General, Powerful, Scalable Graph Transformer** — Ladislav Rampášek, Mikhail Galkin, Vijay Prakash Dwivedi, Anh Tuan Luu, Guy Wolf, Dominique Beaini (NeurIPS 2022, 2022) <https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12454>
  GraphGPS is the strong follow-up that turns graph transformers into a modular recipe: each layer runs a local MPNN and a global attention block in parallel, on top of a menu of positional/structural encodings. Start with the architecture figure and the taxonomy of PEs vs. SEs (local / global / relative) — that taxonomy is the vocabulary the whole field now uses, and it connects directly to the lecture. Note how swapping in linear attention (Performer) makes the model scale to larger graphs, and skim the ablations just enough to see that which encoding matters depends on the dataset — there is no single winner.
**Optional**

- **A Generalization of Transformer Networks to Graphs** — Vijay Prakash Dwivedi, Xavier Bresson (AAAI 2021 Workshop (DLG-AAAI'21), 2021) <https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.09699>
  A short, early graph transformer that predates Graphormer and makes two contrasting design choices: attention restricted to the local neighborhood (sparse) instead of full attention, and Laplacian eigenvectors as positional encodings — the origin of the LapPE idea you see reused in GraphGPS. Read it as a design-space counterpoint: for each choice, ask what Graphormer did differently and why. It is only a few pages, so read it in full.
- **Sign and Basis Invariant Networks for Spectral Graph Representation Learning** — Derek Lim, Joshua Robinson, Lingxiao Zhao, Tess Smidt, Suvrit Sra, Haggai Maron, Stefanie Jegelka (ICLR 2023, 2023) <https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.13013>
  The heavier-theory option: Laplacian eigenvector encodings have a hidden bug — an eigenvector v is only defined up to sign (and, in repeated eigenspaces, up to a change of basis), so a network using raw eigenvectors is not a well-defined function of the graph. Focus on Section 2's statement of these symmetries and the elegant SignNet construction f(v) = rho(phi(v) + phi(-v)); understand why it is sign-invariant by design. Read the universality and expressiveness claims (SignNet subsumes spectral graph convolutions and earlier positional encodings) at the statement level and skip all proofs.
- **Attending to Graph Transformers** — Luis Müller, Mikhail Galkin, Christopher Morris, Ladislav Rampášek (TMLR 2024, 2024) <https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04181>
  A survey that organizes everything from this week into one taxonomy of graph transformer architectures and their structural/positional encodings — use it as a map after reading the two required papers, not before. The most instructive part is the empirical probing: how well graph transformers actually recover graph properties, handle heterophilic graphs, and mitigate over-squashing, which tempers some of the hype. Skim the taxonomy tables closely, and treat the 3D-molecular extensions section as optional.
